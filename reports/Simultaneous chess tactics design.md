# Rebuild Chezz around readable, unequal guesses

Neither combat rule fixes Chezz on its own. A simple one-piece-versus-one-piece payoff model of the current rules shows that at equal HP, **"Always trade" collapses into passivity**, because nobody gains from attacking. **"Free hits" collapses into mutual attack**, because standing still is punished so hard that attacking always dominates. Real guessing with stakes appears only when HP is unequal, when a piece has something worthwhile to do besides fight, and when Defend actually beats an attack. That matches the oldest lesson from simultaneous-move design. Sirlin argues that guessing is interesting only when the options carry **unequal, hard-to-calculate payoffs** ([Sirlin, Designing Yomi](https://www.sirlin.net/articles/designing-yomi)). Hansmann diagnosed earlier synchronous chess variants as either lacking structure, rewarding passive play, or being "a pure gamble" ([Uppsala Synchronous Chess](https://user.it.uu.se/~joachim/PSC)). The closest successful precedents (Into the Breach, Hoplite, Desktop Dungeons, Shotgun King) all pair deterministic combat with visible enemy intent. Chezz's own AI is deterministic and decides from the starting board ([docs/exile-run.md](../docs/exile-run.md)), so in a single-player run its hidden orders are a puzzle the player hasn't been shown yet, not a real opponent's mind. The rebuild should therefore start from a 1v1 payoff harness on top of `exile.ts`. It should choose the combat rule by the matrix it produces rather than by taste, show enemy intent at least as threat zones, and grow one rule at a time using the chess-problem and Nintendo "introduce, develop, twist" discipline. One caveat applies throughout: much of the source material comes from search-result extracts, because direct page fetches were blocked, and the payoff model is the research team's own single-turn calculation, not a published result.

## Every simultaneous chess design needed an explicit conflict rule

Simultaneous play has been tried with chess pieces many times, and each design succeeded or failed on how it settled collisions. Kung-Fu Chess (about 2001) replaced turns with per-piece cooldowns. In an enemy collision, the piece that moved first captures. It won the **2002 IGF Audience Choice award** and later shut down ([Wikipedia: Kung-Fu Chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kung-Fu_Chess)). Kaufman's Simultaneous Randomized Chess runs both moves if they don't interfere, uses the only ordering that works if there is one, and otherwise **discards one move at random** ([jefftk.com](https://www.jefftk.com/p/simultaneous-randomized-chess)). Hutnik's Simultaneous Chess breaks conflicts with an initiative token and suggests revealing the moving piece before its destination ([chessvariants.com](https://chessvariants.com/rules/simultaneous-chess)). Hansmann's Synchronous Chess avoids most conflicts by allowing a move only to a square you "control", meaning one attacked by strictly more of your pieces than the opponent's ([Uppsala](https://user.it.uu.se/~joachim/PSC)). The tiebreakers are timing, randomness, initiative and legality. Randomness is ruled out for Chezz by its own determinism invariant, and Burgun's distinction explains why it fails anyway: noise "injected between a player's choice and the result" does not belong in a strategy game ([Burgun, Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/randomness-and-game-design)).

Diplomacy is the most tested discrete template. Equal-strength entries into one province **bounce**, units cannot swap places, and support adds strength ([Wikibooks Diplomacy/Rules](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Diplomacy/Rules)). Its history is also a warning. Since 1959 it has needed a committee rulebook revision and decades of paradox debate, and its adjudicators conclude that any rule making one unit's success "contingent on the success of some other event during the same season will kill simultaneity and introduce room for paradox" ([diplom.org paradox essay](https://diplom.org/Zine/S1997M/Schwarz/Paradox.html)). Chezz starts in a better position than any of these. HP combat with a single starting snapshot means two pieces that strike each other **both land**, so most chess-style all-or-nothing conflicts disappear ([docs/exile-run.md](../docs/exile-run.md)). Collisions are checked on destination squares only, not along paths. The one existing dependency, "a target vacates only if its move actually succeeds", is a single level deep. That is fine as long as chains (A moves into B's square, B into C's, C is blocked) settle by one fixed-point rule, and every row of the conflict table is written down and tested.

A second family, used by Robo Rally and Mechs vs Minions, plans simultaneously but executes in a fixed priority order. In Robo Rally the robot nearest the antenna acts first ([Wizards 2017 rulebook](https://media.wizards.com/2017/rules/roborally_rules.pdf)). It is easier to read and to code, but robots pushing and blocking each other knock plans off course in ways that feel chaotic ([Tabletop Gaming](https://tabletopgaming.co.uk/reviews/roborally-review)). Chezz's snapshot rule already gives it true simultaneity without that cost, which is a reason to keep it.

## Equal HP turns both combat rules into a solved turn

Each Chezz turn is a normal-form game played from one shared information set: the board at the start of the turn. Sirlin's test for such games is blunt. A simple rock-paper-scissors system works as a strategy game "provided that the rock, paper, and scissors offer unequal risk/rewards", and it is better still if those rewards are unclear ([Sirlin, RPS in Strategy Games](http://sirlingames.squarespace.com/articles/rock-paper-scissors-in-strategy-games.html)). Even unequal payoffs leave the optimal play as a mixed strategy. They are still "a lot better than equal payoffs because at least human nature starts to come into play" ([Sirlin, Designing Yomi](https://www.sirlin.net/articles/designing-yomi)). Turns with a dominant option are solved. Symmetric turns are coin flips. The target is a skewed mix where a good read pays off.

The research team modeled two adjacent pieces. Each secretly picks Attack, Defend, Move away, or Else (do something worth v = 1 elsewhere). The model uses Chezz's rules as documented: damage equals the attacker's current HP, every hit uses starting HP, Always-trade Defend absorbs 1 and retaliates, and a kill is worth K = 2. Equilibria were found by regret matching. This is original analysis, not a published result.

| HP (X v Y) | Always trade equilibrium | Free hits equilibrium |
|---|---|---|
| 3 v 3, v = 1 | Both play **Else** (pure, passive) | Both play **Attack** (pure, mutual destruction) |
| 3 v 3, v = 0 | Both **Defend** (pure, passive) | Both **Attack** |
| 3 v 2, v = 1 | X: Attack 50 / Else 50. Y: Move 50 / Else 50. **Defend never used** | X: Attack 26 / **Defend 24** / Else 50. Y: Attack 25 / Move 75 |
| 3 v 2, v = 2 | Nearly pure (Y always Else, X attacks 10%) | X: Attack 50 / Else 50. Y: Attack 25 / Move 50 / Else 25 |
| 3 v 2, v = 0 | Pure: X attacks, Y flees | Pure: X attacks, Y flees |

The pattern explains the team's debate. **Always trade suppresses attacking.** At equal HP, an attack converts your HP into theirs one-for-one, and Defend's 1-point absorb is too small to matter. In the 3 v 2 case, Else weakly dominates Defend. Play is readable but has little reading in it: the stronger piece attacks, the weaker piece runs, and since a fleeing target makes the attack miss, even that read is often moot. **Free hits rewards initiative.** Because an idle target takes damage without hitting back, it produces an actual cycle: Attack beats Else, Defend and Move neutralize Attack, and Else beats Defend and Move. Defend becomes a real bait for a desperate attack. Its flaw is that equal-HP standoffs resolve as mutual attack, which reads as "trade everything". This is exactly Hansmann's pair of failure modes, passivity and gamble, reproduced in a 4×4 matrix.

Three levers move either rule into the yomi zone, and none of them is the trade-versus-free-hit switch. First, **HP asymmetry** must be common. Current HP doubles as attack strength, so this happens naturally after the first exchange, but it also means lasting king damage can make runs feel hopeless, as the rules doc already warns ([docs/exile-run.md](../docs/exile-run.md)). Second, a real **Else** must exist on most turns: another target, an objective square or a recruit to protect. Without it, not attacking is a default rather than a choice. Third, **Defend must beat a lone attack** outright, for example by taking 0 and returning full damage. That turns Attack-into-Defend from a draw into a loss and makes the cycle strict. A fourth lever, **giving Move a cost**, already exists in the pawn's diagonal hold, which punishes a predicted escape into that square. Frozen Synapse shows the inverse choice: there "a unit that is moving is less likely to win against a stationary one" ([Giant Bomb](https://giantbomb.com/wiki/Games/Frozen_Synapse)). Every simultaneous tactics game has to pick a posture to favor, and should do it on purpose.

The model has clear limits. It is one-on-one and one turn deep, and v and K are arbitrary. The qualitative collapses are robust across the cases tried, but the exact percentages are not, and the researchers found no published trade-versus-free-hit analysis to compare against. That is why the experiment ladder below starts by reproducing this matrix with the real resolver.

## A deterministic AI turns hidden orders into a hidden puzzle

The successful tactics roguelikes chose determinism and visibility deliberately. Subset's Matthew Davis said that FTL "was really reliant" on random chance and that Into the Breach was "a push to be more deterministic in the moment-to-moment gameplay". Justin Ma described simple enemies that announce their attacks so the game is fun "regardless of the AI" ([PC Gamer preview](https://pcgamer.com/into-the-breach-preview)). Telegraphing also sped up play ([Wikipedia: Into the Breach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Into_the_Breach)). Hoplite keeps combat fixed and limits randomness to level generation and upgrades ([IndieRPGs](https://indierpgs.com/2014/12/old-release-hoplite/)). Desktop Dungeons' combat is deterministic as well ([Wurb](https://www.wurb.com/stack/?p=740)). Among chess roguelikes, reviewers praise Shotgun King for "showing who's moving next" and for blocking up to two run-ending moves ([NintendoWorldReport](https://www.nintendoworldreport.com/review/64499)). They criticize randomness that "disrupts the tactical elements" ([KeenGamer](https://www.keengamer.com/articles/reviews/pc-reviews/shotgun-king-the-final-checkmate-review/)). Gambonanza sold 300k copies in three months ([Niche Gamer](https://nichegamer.com/gambonanza-sells-300000-copies-across-steam-and-mobile/)), but its Metacritic score was 64 ([Wikipedia: Gambonanza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambonanza)), and players complain about luck and an AI that makes random or suboptimal moves ([Rogueliker](https://rogueliker.com/gambonanza-review/)). In this genre, the opponent's quality and predictability is the most common weak point.

This creates a tension in Chezz's design. A WeGo game against a human is a yomi game. A WeGo game against a deterministic, "intentionally basic" AI ([docs/exile-run.md](../docs/exile-run.md)) is an Into the Breach puzzle whose telegraph has been hidden. Players will either learn the AI, at which point every turn is effectively telegraphed, or lose to orders they couldn't have reasoned about. Burgun's framing is the right test: information available before the choice behaves like input randomness (fine), while information that arrives only after it behaves like output randomness (bad) ([Burgun](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/randomness-and-game-design)). The middle grounds are well documented. Hutnik reveals the piece before the destination ([chessvariants.com](https://chessvariants.com/rules/simultaneous-chess)). Frozen Synapse lets players script hypothetical enemy orders to test their own plan ([Giant Bomb](https://www.giantbomb.com/games/3030-26505/)). Threat-zone overlays show every square an enemy could strike or hold. Showing an intent category (attack, hold, move) without the target keeps a reading layer while removing "I didn't know that was possible."

Player complaints about WeGo point the same way. Reviewers call "unexpected failure" Frozen Synapse 2's "bread and butter" and list a "frustrating UI" as a con ([Trusted Reviews](https://trustedreviews.com/reviews/frozen-synapse-2)). Strategy players say they want to "see the effects of my actions immediately", and large battles overwhelm them with orders ([Stardock forums](https://forums.sorcererking.com/383074/a-better-combat-system-we-go)). Combat Mission players, on the other hand, call the replay "the most 'fun' part" ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1369370/discussions/0/2946998508810164701)). Chezz already has the cures: small boards, at most three orders, and playback that cannot change outcomes. What it needs is for playback to *explain* each conflict, so a player can always name the enemy order that beat them and why it was plausible from the starting board.

## Chess problems already define what a good tiny board looks like

A composed chess problem has an explicit quality standard that maps almost one-to-one onto a "win in N turns" Chezz encounter. A problem with two winning first moves is "cooked" and would not be published ([The Problemist](https://mail.theproblemist.org/beginner.pl?type=b_int)). Good keys are quiet and rarely captures. Economy means no unused material, space or motivation. Plausible "tries" that fail to exactly one defence make the real key satisfying ([chess.com, Rocky64](https://www.chess.com/blog/Rocky64/an-introduction-to-composed-chess-problems)). In simultaneous Chezz, the key is the player's whole order set for turn 1, and a refutation is an enemy order set. So a puzzle must either win against **every** enemy reply (chess-problem style) or against a **shown** enemy plan (Into the Breach style). Each encounter should say which. The quiet keys are where Chezz's distinctive "aha" lives: stepping out of a line so a simultaneous hit lands, or accepting a hit because damage resolves from starting HP.

Small boards make this verifiable rather than aspirational. Gardner's 5×5 minichess, with about 9×10^18 positions, was weakly solved as a draw with modest computing power ([Mhalla & Prost, arXiv 1307.7118](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.7118)). Passant's early demo shipped a Camel placement that forced mate "nearly every time" until the developer restricted it ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3353100/discussions/0/604146775975003760)). That is the kind of degenerate opening an exhaustive search catches before players do. The procedural-generation literature describes the matching pipeline: generate candidate boards, test them with a solver, and keep only those passing crisp yes/no constraints ([Togelius et al.](https://course.ccs.neu.edu/cs5150f13/readings/togelius_sbpcg.pdf)). The Refraction work adds a filter that rejects puzzles solvable by a shortcut that bypasses the target concept ([Smith et al. 2013](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/smith2013qop.pdf)). Search theory also shows how to make the solver honest in a simultaneous game. Each node is a matrix game, and regret-matching MCTS converges toward Nash equilibrium where plain UCT stays exploitable ([Lanctot, Lisý & Winands 2013](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/monte-carlo-tree-search-in-simultaneous-move-games-with-applicati/); [Lisý et al., arXiv 1310.8613](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.8613v1)). Such a solver doubles as a balance instrument: any option it never mixes in is dominated.

Teaching follows the same economy. Nintendo's Koichi Hayashida builds each level around one concept: present it, let players develop it, then "throw them for a loop" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-secret-to-i-mario-i-level-design)). Arvi Teikari wants almost every Baba Is You level to produce "some kind of a 'realization'" ([Game Pilgrim](https://gamepilgrim.com/2019/04/06/developer-interview-hempuli-oy/)). Portal's chambers each start from "one key aspect of a mechanic" ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/17/thinking-with-portals-making-a-test-chamber)). Alan Hazelden prototyped A Good Snowman in PuzzleScript before writing any code, to test whether the mechanics allowed interesting puzzles at all ([Game Developer Q&A](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-a-good-snowman-is-hard-to-build)). Kim Swift called playtesting "probably the most important thing we did on Portal" and advised watching people play rather than reading reports ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/10-years-of-design-lessons-from-em-portal-em-s-kim-swift)). For Chezz, the cheap prototype is a paper board with a facilitator resolving written orders, a classic Wizard-of-Oz setup ([MIT CMS.611 handout](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/cms-611j-creating-video-games-fall-2014/348ee58d265e61e4760d196bd97b1ad1_MITCMS_611JF14_Paper_Prot.pdf)). With about five testers per round ([UWaterloo CS449](https://student.cs.uwaterloo.ca/~cs449/s17/Lecture%2010%20slides.pdf)), that is enough to separate rule confusion from search difficulty.

The roguelike layer has its own precedents. Really Bad Chess kept tactics while removing opening knowledge, and Gage found that pure randomness "doesn't feel like a game", so armies are generated under light constraints ([Vice/Motherboard](https://motherboard.vice.com/read/really-bad-chess-is-a-really-fun-insult-to-chess)). Shotgun King's paired help/harm upgrade cards tie each power gain to a harder next floor, but players resent forced pairs that can end a run ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1972440/discussions/0/3551679689850940322)). Its "souls" (move once like a piece you defeated) are a light form of recruitment that suits an exile theme ([StackUp](https://www.stackup.org/post/review-shotgun-king-the-final-checkmate)). Chezz's recruited army is its structural advantage over lone-hero games: forks, discovered attacks and body-blocking need several friendly pieces. With HP, though, a fork is only real when both threats are lethal, so damage previews must mark lethal versus non-lethal. One practical note: a mobile game called "Chezz: Play Fast Chess" by Appside, a Kung-Fu Chess derivative with an upgrade-based adventure mode, already exists ([TouchArcade](https://toucharcade.com/games/chezz-play-fast-chess)).

## Conclusion

The trade-versus-free-hit debate is the wrong unit of decision. Both rules are defensible, and both fail at equal HP with nothing else to do. What decides whether a Chezz turn is a read, a coin flip or a formality is the *shape of the matrix*: whether Defend beats Attack, whether Move has a cost, whether an "Else" exists, and how often HP is lopsided. The team should therefore stop comparing whole runs and start comparing matrices, with the real resolver producing them. The second shift is to admit that a single-player run against a deterministic AI is a puzzle game wearing WeGo clothes. That is not a weakness, since it is exactly the Into the Breach formula, but the interface should be as honest as the rules, and the yomi layer is better earned through partial intent display and lopsided stakes than through hiding a predictable AI. Open questions remain: no source measures perceived fairness across intent-disclosure levels, the payoff numbers are untested beyond 1v1, and the ideal element budget per puzzle must come from Chezz's own playtests.

## Design principles

| # | Principle | Grounding |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Judge every rule by the matrix it creates on a typical turn.** Reject rules that leave one option above roughly 70% of equilibrium play (dominant), or that produce a uniform mix (coin flip). | Sirlin's unequal payoffs; the payoff model above |
| 2 | **Make counter-relations clear and payoffs unequal.** Players should know that Attack beats Idle, Move beats Attack, Hold beats Move into the diagonal, and Defend beats Attack. HP, lethality and position should make the stakes lopsided and state-dependent. | Sirlin; forum counterpoint that uncertainty belongs in the read, not the rules |
| 3 | **Defend must win against a lone attack**, or it will be dominated or ignored. | Defend is never used under Always trade in the model |
| 4 | **Every turn needs a real "Else"** (another target, an objective, a piece to protect) so that holding back is a choice. | Equilibria collapse when v = 0 |
| 5 | **Fleeing must cost something** (walls, pawn holds, tempo), or it answers every attack. | Pawn hold as the counter to Move |
| 6 | **Keep resolution order-independent and deterministic.** No random tiebreaks, and no outcome that depends on another order's success beyond one documented fixed-point rule. Write the full conflict table and test every row. | Burgun; Diplomacy paradoxes; Kaufman's random discard |
| 7 | **Show at least threat zones, and preferably intent categories**, while the AI is deterministic. Never let a loss come from an order the player couldn't have reasoned about. | Into the Breach; Burgun's input/output spectrum |
| 8 | **Playback must explain, not just show.** Label why each conflict resolved as it did, and let players pause and step through. | Frozen Synapse 2 complaints; Combat Mission replay praise |
| 9 | **One new idea per encounter**: introduce, develop, twist, combine, on the smallest board that works. | Hayashida, Portal, Teikari |
| 10 | **Every authored encounter passes a solver.** Exactly one turn-1 key (no cook), no shorter win, the concept is necessary, every element is necessary, and the puzzle is committed as a regression test. | Chess problem conventions; Refraction; repo's test-first rule |
| 11 | **Use light generation rules, not raw randomness**, for boards, armies and recruits. Offer a way out of any forced bad pairing. | Really Bad Chess; Shotgun King complaints |
| 12 | **Mark lethal versus non-lethal on every preview**, so that forks and trades can be read at a glance. | HP chess readability; snapshot rule |

## Experiment ladder from one piece upward

Each experiment adds one variable. "Harness" means a TypeScript script that imports the pure resolver in `src/game/exile.ts`, enumerates every joint order set, and computes equilibria by regret matching. Reusing the game's own resolver keeps the solver and the game from drifting apart. "Paper test" means a Wizard-of-Oz session with 3–5 players who write orders secretly while a facilitator resolves them.

### Stage 0: instrument before designing

**E0. Can the harness reproduce the hand-built matrix?** *Question:* do the real rules match the model? *Setup:* two kings, adjacent, on an open 3×3 patch of a 4×4 board, at HP 3 v 3 and 3 v 2, under both combat modes. The orders are Attack, Defend, Move to each legal square, and a scripted Else worth v. *Measure:* the payoff matrix and equilibrium, compared with the table above. *Changes the design if:* the real rules diverge from the model, for example because a Move-to-square option or the stuck-piece rule creates a different equilibrium. In that case, the documented rules aren't what the team thinks they are, and `docs/exile-run.md` gets corrected before anything else.

### Stage 1: one piece versus one piece, one rule at a time

**E1. Does the combat rule alone produce a dominant option?** *Question:* across all HP pairs, where does each rule collapse? *Setup:* the E0 harness swept over HP 1–5 for each side and v ∈ {0, 1, 2}, for both modes. *Measure:* a heat map of the largest equilibrium probability on any single option, the share of cells with a pure equilibrium, and the game value. *Changes the design if:* one mode keeps every option below 70% across most asymmetric cells while the other doesn't. That settles the trade-versus-free-hit question on evidence. If both collapse in most cells, neither rule is shippable without the levers in E2–E4.

**E2. How strong must Defend be to complete the cycle?** *Question:* which Defend variant makes Attack, Defend, Move and Else a strict cycle? *Setup:* hold E1's grid and vary only Defend: absorb 0, absorb 1 (current), take 0 and return full, or return +1 bonus. *Measure:* how often Defend is in the equilibrium support, the reduction in pure-equilibrium cells, and whether Attack into Defend becomes a loss. *Changes the design if:* only a strong Defend keeps it in play. Then adopt it and update the rules doc test-first. If no variant helps under Always trade, that is decisive evidence against Always trade.

**E3. Does making flight costly restore the value of attacking?** *Question:* is free escape the reason Always trade stalls? *Setup:* the same duel with escape squares limited to 0, 1 or 2 by walls, then with an enemy pawn holding one escape diagonal. *Measure:* Attack frequency, Move frequency and game value. *Changes the design if:* limiting flight makes attacking common under Always trade. Then board geometry, not the combat rule, is the main lever, and encounters should be authored around escape-square counts.

**E4. Can people read the duel?** *Question:* does the theoretical cycle feel like reading the opponent or like guessing? *Setup:* a paper test of the E2 winner and the current rule, best of 10 duels per pair, with one player as the opponent. *Measure:* how often players correctly predict the outcome before reveal, whether they can name and justify the opponent's order afterwards, and a self-report of "read or coin flip" on a 5-point scale. *Changes the design if:* players call the more mixed rule a coin flip. Then the payoffs are not legible enough, and HP and lethal previews must be made more prominent before adding complexity.

### Stage 2: simultaneity's own rules

**E5. Is every conflict row legible and order-independent?** *Question:* do same-square entries, swaps, attacks on a leaving piece, chained moves and pawn holds all resolve predictably? *Setup:* write the full conflict table as `tests/exile.test.ts` cases (2–3 pieces each), and include a permutation test asserting that the result doesn't depend on order processing. Then show each case to paper testers before playback. *Measure:* test pass rate, permutation invariance, and the human prediction rate per row. *Changes the design if:* any row fails invariance (fix it before going further), or any row falls below about 60% human prediction. Simplify that rule, for example by making a contested square a bounce as in Diplomacy.

**E6. How much enemy intent should be shown?** *Question:* with a deterministic AI, which disclosure level gives readable outcomes without making the game a solved puzzle? *Setup:* the same three 1v2 encounters played under four displays: hidden, threat zones, intent category, and exact intent. Each player sees two of them, counterbalanced. *Measure:* turn time, win rate, "could you explain each hit?", "read or coin flip", and whether players reverse-engineer the AI within 3 encounters. *Changes the design if:* hidden intent scores worst on explainability without adding perceived depth. Then threat zones become the minimum. If exact intent makes win rate jump and turn time collapse, prefer intent categories.

### Stage 3: two and three pieces, the first real tactics

**E7. Does a second friendly piece create chess tactics?** *Question:* do forks, body-blocks and support emerge under HP rules? *Setup:* 2v1 and 2v2 on 4×4 with one wall. Use the harness to enumerate positions where a single order set threatens two lethal hits, then repeat with damage reduced so one threat is non-lethal. *Measure:* the share of positions with a forcing line, and how often players spot the fork with and without lethal markers. *Changes the design if:* forks rarely force anything because HP buffers absorb them. Then lower HP pools (1–4) or add the lethal preview before building larger armies.

**E8. Can the snapshot rule carry a puzzle on its own?** *Question:* is "a dying piece still hits" an aha or a confusion? *Setup:* a 4×4 introduce/develop/twist trio where the only win relies on the snapshot rule. The key must be unique against every enemy reply, as confirmed by the solver. *Measure:* solve time, wrong attempts, the first order tried, and whether players can explain why it worked. *Changes the design if:* players solve it but can't explain it. That is rule confusion, so fix the playback labels. If they can't solve it after seeing the rule, the twist is too early in the curve.

### Stage 4: encounters and generation

**E9. Can the solver filter produce good encounters?** *Question:* do the cook, concept and economy checks keep boards that people enjoy? *Setup:* generate and test about 200 4×4 and 5×5 boards with at most 3 units per side, N ≤ 3, and either telegraphed or adversarial enemies. Keep only boards with a unique key, a necessary concept and necessary elements. *Measure:* solver nodes, tries and branching, correlated against human solve time and rated fun for 10 survivors and 5 rejects. *Changes the design if:* the soft metrics don't predict human difficulty. Then authored encounters stay hand-built, with the solver acting only as a gate. If exhaustive search is intractable at 5×5, cap generation at 4×4.

**E10. Does the AI matter more than the rules?** *Question:* is the basic deterministic AI exploitable enough to hide rule quality? *Setup:* run the current AI against a regret-matching AI on 20 encounters under the chosen combat rule. *Measure:* the win-rate gap, and options the equilibrium AI never uses. *Changes the design if:* the gap is large. Then playtest verdicts about combat rules have been measuring AI weakness. Either telegraph the basic AI as an Into the Breach-style puzzle opponent, or give it readable "temperaments".

### Stage 5: the run

**E11. Does carried damage make runs hopeless?** *Question:* given that HP is also attack strength, does early king damage snowball? *Setup:* simulate 1,000 runs of the three-encounter slice with the E10 AI standing in for the player, under both the persistent-HP and full-heal-between-fights variants, then a paper test with five players. *Measure:* the distribution of king HP entering each encounter, win rate conditioned on HP lost in encounter 1, and whether healing is ever chosen over recruiting. *Changes the design if:* losing more than about 40% of king HP early makes later encounters near-unwinnable. Then decouple attack strength from current HP for the king, or add Shotgun King-style protection against run-ending moves.

**E12. Does recruitment change plans rather than just adding stats?** *Question:* do recruits create new tactics (forks, blocks) or only more HP? *Setup:* the same encounter played with each recruit option (bishop, rook, heal, save), using the solver to list each option's winning lines. *Measure:* how many distinct winning-line types each recruit enables, and player-reported plan changes. *Changes the design if:* one recruit dominates every encounter, which is the Passant Camel lesson. Then restrict it by placement or cost, or redesign encounters so each recruit has a board where it is the key.
